Deciding what “right” looks like

Six rounds of local experiments turned “make it look good” — which no model can act on — into a written specification precise enough that the final render came back right the first time.

Read the full findings

Round 1. Measured with SSIM: on a painted image the picture starts changing at denoise 0.45; on a flat-coloured one it holds to 0.60. The encoder alone caps similarity at 0.98–0.996.

Round 2. An edit model given “this face from that other picture” brings that picture's expression and lighting with it, and the fast 8-step variant drifts to one default face.

Round 3. Words-only kept everything else intact; the attached colour reference dragged its own character's hair colour and braid into the image — and the wrong result scored SSIM 0.958 against 0.965 for the right one.

Round 4. Identity and outfit held perfectly, but the body stopped at about six heads. Pushing harder (full model, higher guidance) finally moved the body and broke the character: hair colour changed, the pinafore became a pencil skirt, the linework turned Western.

Round 5. 15 separated clearly; 20 and 25 came back nearly identical. Asking for “mature” only added makeup.

Round 6. A newer base model gave correct adult proportions on the first attempt, 12–24 seconds per image. Reference images alone still failed three ways: ignored entirely, turned into a turnaround sheet, or the outfit replaced.

The specification

Four blocks, so only one changes at a time

01

Identity — exact

Every defining element named: hairstyle and its ribbon, eye colour, each garment's cut, colour and details. Anything vague gets redrawn next time.

02

Proportions — structure

Seven to seven and a half heads; small head, long legs, defined waist; adult jaw, eyes at natural size. Not chibi, not fashion-elongated.

03

Pose — one sentence

Relaxed three-quarter stance, weight on one leg, one hand at her hair. Everything else stays byte-identical between poses.

04

Rendering — what looks drawn

Clean lineart, painterly shading, no plastic gloss, fabric that differs by material, detailed hands, soft studio light, whole body in frame.

Deliberately left out, because the rounds showed it doesn't help: quality-tag stacking, long negative-prompt lists, style references for colour work, and the word “mature” on its own.

The check that survived

The score that couldn't see the error, and the one that could

The character's colours are clustered and compared with the approved design. A drifted design is caught automatically — on white-background sheets. On busy scene art it's too noisy, and a person looks.

  • Dropped: similarity scoring (SSIM) as a gate — it rated a wrong-coloured result 0.958 against 0.965 for the right one
  • Kept: a palette check that separates same-character from different-character by a factor of three
  • The final render was handed to the strongest hosted image model available. The transferable work is the specification and the acceptance criteria — knowing what to ask for, and how to tell whether you got it

Status & limits

What exists, what's measured, what isn't claimed

Built

  • A four-block character specification that reproduces the same character across poses
  • A palette check that flags design drift automatically
  • Six recorded rounds, one variable each, with parameters and conclusions

Measured

  • Denoise thresholds per image type (0.45 painted, 0.60 flat)
  • Encoder similarity ceiling: 0.98–0.996
  • Palette distance: 3.8–6.6 same character vs 19.0–20.3 different character

Not claimed

  • The final illustrations were not rendered locally
  • The palette check is unusable on busy scene art
  • No public repo — the scripts stay private
