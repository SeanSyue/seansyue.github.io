Tetherlock

A local workspace that stays shut unless three things hold at once — and closes again the moment one of them stops holding. Tetherlock ties access to a physical device: take the device away, and the workspace is no longer yours to read.

Overview

Access that leaves with you

The problem

A password follows the machine, not the person. Anyone at an unlocked computer inherits it, and it keeps working long after the person has walked away. The right to read should be something you carry — and can take back.

  • My role: the threat model, the unlock and lock rules, how licences are issued and verified, where each key lives
  • AI wrote most of the implementation against those rules; the rules and what counts as done are mine
  • Built for my own use; never handed to any employer or client

The rule, in one line

Account qualifies+Licensed device present+Password passes→Workspace opens
Device removed→Workspace locks · further actions refused
01

Three conditions, no substitutes

Eligibility, device and password are checked together. Holding two of the three opens nothing — there is no fallback path that trades one for another.

02

Checked continuously, not once

Access is a state, not a door that stays open. The session keeps verifying; when the device goes, the workspace locks and later actions are refused rather than queued.

03

Maintenance without handing over the keys

Someone can be given a narrow, time-limited right to maintain the system — bounded in both what it can do and how long it lasts — instead of borrowing full access.

How it works

Opening, staying open, and closing

Opening

  1. The account must qualify. Being a user of the machine is not enough; the account has to be one this workspace recognises.
  2. The licensed device must be present and valid. The licence is checked, not just the presence of some device — an expired, altered or unrecognised licence is refused.
  3. The password must pass. The usual thing the person knows.
  4. The device is verified again, after the password. The check that matters is the state at the moment of opening, not the state when the screen appeared.

Staying open

An open workspace keeps re-asserting the same conditions. Nothing about the first successful unlock is treated as permanent, so a session cannot outlive the reason it was allowed.

Closing

Removing the device revokes access: the workspace locks, and operations attempted afterwards are refused. Locking is the default reaction to losing a condition — the system closes rather than asking whether it should.

Delegated maintenance

Maintenance is a separate, lesser grant: limited in capability, limited in time, and distinct from the owner's own access. It exists so that "someone needs to fix this" never becomes a reason to share the real credentials.

Design decisions

Three calls, and why

01

Re-check the device after the password

Checking once, then showing a prompt, leaves a window in which the device can be swapped while the person types. The decision uses the state at the moment of opening. Cost: a slightly slower unlock.

02

Separate keys; only the issuer signs

A component that only needs to check a licence never holds what would let it write one. One key for everything is one compromise that ends everything. Cost: more key material to rotate.

03

Lock by default when a condition disappears

No “are you still there?”, no grace window, no queued operations waiting for the device to return. Cost: the legitimate owner occasionally unlocks again. That is the right side to err on.

Scope & limits

What it does not claim

A security design is only as honest as its boundary. Tetherlock controls access to a workspace inside an application. It is not a disk encryption product, and it does not make a device impossible to copy.

Designed and built

  • Three-condition unlock
  • Continuous checking with lock on device removal
  • Time- and capability-limited delegated maintenance
  • Separate keys per purpose; issuing kept apart from verifying

Deliberately out of scope

  • Encryption of data at rest
  • Resistance to cloning a device identifier
  • Protecting a machine whose operating system is already compromised
  • Anything about memory being wiped on lock

Not claimed here

  • No recording or third-party audit
  • No test numbers on this page; the standalone version and its own tests are on GitHub
  • Not packaged for anyone else's use
